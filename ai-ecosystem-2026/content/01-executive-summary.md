# Executive Summary

Armenia has a small but substantive AI ecosystem. It combines active academic and industrial research groups, a varied product and services sector, multi-year AI education in public high schools, university and non-degree technical programs, a dense community centered in Yerevan, and a rapidly expanding base of local GPU infrastructure. These components are real, but they differ greatly in scale, maturity, and the strength of the public evidence behind them.

## Armenia at a glance

| Dimension | Current picture |
|---|---|
| **Research** | **23 active research groups** are mapped across core, industrial, and applied AI; **103 named publications** are cited. |
| **Industry** | **114 organizations** are profiled across AI products, consulting and services, and internal AI teams. |
| **Education** | Available 2026 admissions data cover **487 Generation AI admissions**, **460 undergraduate admissions**, and **88 master's admissions** across programs with usable data. |
| **Compute** | YSU, Eleveight AI, and Firebird operate **6,736 publicly documented data-center GPUs** as of August 2026. |
| **Government** | The government funds AI research and public compute, coordinates school integration, subsidizes access to commercial infrastructure, supports industry, and is beginning to deploy and govern AI in public services. |
| **Community and literacy** | Recurring conferences, reading groups, meetups, summer schools, and specialist online groups connect researchers and practitioners. AI literacy is about learning to use AI tools, not developing AI systems. |

## Principal findings

1. **Research capacity exists, but world-class output is very limited.** Armenia has identifiable groups and internationally visible work, including core AI research and AI-enabled science, but sustained research at NeurIPS and comparable top-tier venues depends on very few groups and supervisors.
2. **There is no dominant AI specialization; instead, Armenia has several overlapping, mid-sized clusters.** The 114 profiled organizations span many application areas. Voice and speech, document intelligence, and life sciences are among the clearest established clusters, while physical AI is beginning to emerge across robotics, autonomy, perception, and embedded systems.
3. **Education has scale, but its advanced research output is not yet world-class.** Large numbers of students enter through school, degree, professional, and enrichment programs. Armenia nevertheless has no dedicated high-quality AI PhD program and very few supervisors with sustained records of world-class AI research and NeurIPS-level student supervision. YSU has the strongest emerging institutional base because its programs connect to several research groups and the national GPU cluster.
4. **Compute has moved from scarcity to utilization.** Public, private, and commercial platforms provide distinct access routes, with 6,736 operational data-center GPUs documented in August 2026. Hardware totals alone do not establish domestic benefit; utilization by Armenia-based users is an important metric to track alongside access, affordability, support, and resulting outputs.
5. **Government support is substantive but insufficient to maintain world-class labs and programs.** Research grants, public and subsidized compute, school programs, industry support, and early public-sector AI deployments are visible, but sustained funding and coordination are still needed to maintain stronger teams and institutions.
6. **Community connections and AI literacy widen participation.** Events, specialist groups, international ties, and access to AI tools broaden the ecosystem and connect students, researchers, engineers, and founders.

The chapters that follow examine Armenia's research groups, education pathways, computing infrastructure, companies, government support, professional communities, and AI-literacy programs, before assessing how these components can develop into stronger institutions and world-class outcomes.
